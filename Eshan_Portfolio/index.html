Every system I've built started the same way — a question I couldn't leave alone.

My grandfather was a civil engineer — he taught me to break big problems into solvable puzzles. My father is a farmer and a lecturer, and he showed me how knowledge and ground reality have to meet before anything meaningful happens. Between the two of them, I got a strange, useful inheritance: think in systems, but never forget who it's actually for.

I wrote my first lines of code on a Samsung E3 — a basic Android phone, no real dev tools, just curiosity and a willingness to break things. That curiosity turned physical in 9th grade, when I built a production-grade automatic aquarium maintenance system — feeding, lighting, power backup — that ran independently for six-plus weeks during the COVID-19 lockdown. That's where IoT got under my skin.

"If something is important enough, you should try even if the probable outcome is failure." — a philosophy borrowed from Elon Musk, and one I actually live by
